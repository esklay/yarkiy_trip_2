A few kilometers from the Step Pyramid of Djoser at Saqqara, deep beneath the sandy plateau, lies one of the most mysterious and least understood underground complexes of Ancient Egypt — the Serapeum.

When French archaeologist Auguste Mariette first cleared the entrance to these underground catacombs in 1850, he expected to find a traditional necropolis burial. What the explorers discovered, however, still sparks debate among historians, engineers, and Egyptologists around the world.

The narrow underground galleries of the Serapeum with niches carved for monolithic sarcophagi.

25 Giants of Monolithic Granite

The Serapeum is a network of narrow underground corridors carved directly into the limestone bedrock. Along these corridors lie deep niches, each containing a giant sarcophagus.

The complex contains a total of 24 monolithic granite sarcophagi (plus one abandoned in a narrow corridor). Here are just a few facts that make any engineer stop and think:

  • Weight: Each sarcophagus with its lid weighs between 70 and 100 tons.
  • Material: High-strength Aswan granite and diorite, quarried 800 kilometers south, up the Nile.
  • Geometry: Inner and outer surfaces polished to a mirror finish, with angular tolerances of fractions of a millimeter.
"The main question is not why they were built, but how — under the conditions of narrow underground tunnels barely two meters wide — it was possible to bring in and perfectly install a 100-ton mass without modern equipment."

The Official Version vs. Engineers' Questions

According to traditional academic Egyptology, the Serapeum was a necropolis for the sacred Apis bulls — earthly incarnations of the god Ptah, and later Osiris. Legend has it that after a bull's death, it was mummified and buried with honors in a granite sarcophagus.

However, when Mariette used dynamite to open one of the sealed sarcophagi, it was found to be absolutely empty — there were no bull mummies, no gold, no burial artifacts, only a layer of bituminous residue.

Modern builders and engineers visiting the Serapeum note the perfect straightness of the sarcophagi's internal corners (exactly 90 degrees) — precision that today can only be achieved with high-precision CNC milling machines.
